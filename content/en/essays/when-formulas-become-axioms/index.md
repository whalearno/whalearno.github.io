*Subtitle: Rethinking the Lorentz Transformation, Quantum Measurement, and the Structure of Physical Interactions*

**Author:** Chih-Ming Wang

## Introduction: When Successful Formulas Become the Starting Points of Thought

Some of the most fascinating moments in the history of science are not simply those in which a formula is finally shown to work. They are the moments when a phenomenon that once seemed difficult to understand suddenly becomes intelligible through a remarkably simple mathematical relationship.

Newton brought celestial and terrestrial mechanics together through his laws of motion. Einstein used the principle of relativity to reconsider time and space. Quantum theory established extraordinarily accurate predictions of the microscopic world using states and operators in Hilbert space. These breakthroughs transformed how humanity understands nature. Yet when a mathematical representation becomes successful enough to serve as the common language of later research, something intriguing can happen: **the starting points introduced to make sense of particular difficulties gradually come to be regarded as self-evident structures of nature itself.**

I call this the *naturalization of axioms*. I use *ontological debt* as a metaphor for the questions it leaves behind: we know how to calculate with a structure, yet we can still ask why that structure holds, and from what physical relationships it might arise.

An explanation that merely rewrites an old assumption in new symbols has not taught us very much. More interesting progress would show that conditions once introduced independently have a common origin, or would reveal constraints and testable relationships that the earlier description did not make visible.

This curiosity also motivates MST-EUT: **behind formulas that already work exceptionally well, might there be relationships of interaction that we have not yet fully learned to see?**

## 1. The Crossroads of 1905: One Set of Formulas, Three Physical Understandings

The Lorentz transformation did not first appear in the familiar guise of a “fundamental symmetry of spacetime.” At the end of the nineteenth century, electromagnetic theory, the ether hypothesis, and optical experiments stood in an uneasy relationship. If light required a medium through which to propagate, why could the expected ether drift not be detected?

Lorentz sought an answer in electromagnetism and in the dynamical effects of moving matter. Poincaré went further in recognizing the group structure of the transformations, while bringing the principle of relativity and synchronization by light signals into the discussion. In 1905, Einstein approached the problem from another direction. Taking the principle of relativity and the constancy of the speed of light in vacuum as his starting points, he reorganized the measurement relationships among rulers, clocks, and light signals. Minkowski subsequently gave these relationships a geometric expression in four-dimensional spacetime.

| Thinker | Central question | Understanding of the transformation |
|---|---|---|
| **Lorentz** | Why was the expected ether drift not observed? | Within an electromagnetic theory of the ether, the results were understood through the dynamics of matter, length contraction, and local time. |
| **Poincaré** | How could the principle of relativity become a universal physical law? | He emphasized transformation groups, electron models, and the physical interpretation of local time through synchronization with light signals. |
| **Einstein (1905)** | Could a consistent kinematics be established without first knowing the microscopic constitution of matter? | Beginning with operational measurement relationships and two postulates, he derived length contraction, time dilation, and the relativity of simultaneity. |

What makes this history so thought-provoking is not the question of which scientist truly “owned” the Lorentz transformation. It is that **different ways of asking physical questions could converge on the same mathematical relationships.**

Einstein's approach succeeded precisely because it did not have to wait for a complete microscopic dynamics of a medium before establishing a reliable kinematic structure. Yet as length contraction, time dilation, and the speed limit came to seem like the natural language of spacetime geometry, the deeper question remained compelling: why does nature exhibit this particular causal structure? Why do different physical processes obey it together?

In 1919, Einstein distinguished between *principle theories* and *constructive theories*. The former begin with general principles and derive constraints that phenomena must satisfy. The latter seek to explain how phenomena arise from more elementary physical processes. Both approaches have their place in physics, and each offers a different level of understanding.

A principle can be extraordinarily successful, and the question of its origin can remain worth asking.

## 2. The Closing Effect of Axiomatization and the Methodological Boomerang

Every major theoretical breakthrough brings a new convenience: phenomena that once required elaborate explanations can now be derived from a few axioms. This allows physics to advance, but it also changes the kinds of questions researchers habitually ask.

In relativity, Lorentz structure tells us how the speed of light, time, and causality fit together. But why should nature have a finite and universal causal speed limit in the first place? General relativity gives gravity a dynamical metric, but where does the smooth geometric description cease to apply under extreme conditions? The Standard Model organizes interactions through the gauge structure \(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\), but why this particular collection of groups and representations rather than another possible combination?

Once these questions are expressed as precise mathematical conditions, they become all the more worth investigating. Symmetry can tell us which interactions are allowed. It also invites a further question: do those conditions of possibility themselves have an origin?

Looking back at Einstein and the history of quantum mechanics, we find an almost ironic *methodological boomerang*.

The young Einstein was influenced by Ernst Mach's empirical criticism and placed great importance on operational measurement relationships. Yet his commitment to the reality of atoms also shows why his thinking cannot be reduced to uncompromising positivism. In 1905, by no longer relying on a difficult-to-verify picture of a mechanical ether, he gave relativity an unusually clear starting point.

In the 1920s, quantum mechanics began describing microscopic phenomena with astonishing precision. Parts of the Copenhagen tradition increasingly prioritized the calculation of observable outcomes, while leaving the nature of microscopic reality before measurement open or suspended. In 1989, David Mermin captured this cultural tendency in the now-famous phrase, “Shut up and calculate!”

What is interesting is that, later in life, Einstein became one of the leading voices asking about physical reality beyond observation when confronted with probabilistic interpretations of quantum mechanics. In his 1920 Leiden lecture, he also returned to the language of an “ether” in a broader, non-mechanical sense, to discuss the physical properties that general relativity attributes to space.

Lorentz, Einstein, and the quantum theorists faced different problems. Still, their stories form a striking historical parallel: **a way of thinking that once helped us move beyond an old difficulty may itself become something we need to question in a later age.**

## 3. From Special to General Relativity: Geometric Rigidity and Feedback

Special relativity (SR) and general relativity (GR) are mathematically closely compatible. The local inertial structure of GR retains the Lorentz form familiar from SR, while its flat limit brings back Minkowski geometry.

But when we ask how these mathematical structures come about, the two theories present different physical pictures.

| Aspect | Special relativity (SR) | General relativity (GR) |
|---|---|---|
| **Spacetime geometry** | The Minkowski metric is fixed, and physical processes unfold within it. | The metric participates in the dynamics and couples to the energy and momentum of matter. |
| **Energy and momentum** | Translation symmetry gives global conserved quantities a clear role; in inertial coordinates, \(\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0\). | Local covariant conservation takes the form \(\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0\); the definition of global energy depends on symmetries and boundary conditions. |
| **Inertial structure** | Global inertial frames can be used. | Inertial coordinates can be chosen locally, but curvature generally cannot be removed throughout a region by a change of coordinates. |

The intriguing question here is not whether the two theories contradict each other. It is this: **could a fixed inertial geometry and a dynamical geometry capable of feedback be different manifestations of the same more fundamental interactions under different conditions?**

Condensed-matter physics offers an intuitive way to imagine such a possibility. A system in a uniform ground state may display stable and remarkably simple effective propagation laws. When the system is disturbed, its density, couplings, and collective modes can change, and so can its macroscopic propagation structure. This brings to mind the fixed structure of SR and the curvature feedback of GR, almost as if we were viewing the same underlying interactions under two different sets of conditions.

Yet condensed-matter systems also expose the hardest part of this picture. Ordinary fluids and crystals tend to have preferred reference frames, and different excitations can propagate at different speeds. **Why, then, does nature allow different physical channels to share such a precise Lorentzian causal structure?**

That question turns the metaphor “is spacetime like a fluid?” into a more concrete research direction: searching for interactions and constraints that produce a shared causal structure in the uniform limit while allowing geometric feedback under nonuniform conditions.

## 4. Four Questions Quantum Physics Leaves Us With

The experimental success of quantum theory and the Standard Model can make it easy to forget that they also leave us with several different kinds of foundational questions.

**Measurement: When does a probability become one actual outcome?** The evolution of a quantum state and the rules of measurement projection form a powerful predictive framework. Environmental decoherence helps explain why quantum interference becomes difficult to preserve under macroscopic conditions and gives a more concrete account of the connection between quantum and classical behavior. Yet “why does this particular outcome occur in a single measurement?” still takes us to the heart of quantum interpretation.

**Parameters: Why these particular values?** The Standard Model describes elementary particles and interactions with remarkable precision, but masses, mixings, and coupling parameters must still be supplied by experiment. The minimal version is commonly said to have about 19 free parameters; including neutrino masses and mixing adds more. The existence of three generations of fermions, and their pattern of masses, adds another layer of unexplained selectivity to this elegant algebraic structure.

**Quantum gravity: How does geometry itself enter a quantum description?** Quantum field theory typically studies quantum fields on a given background geometry, and quantum field theory in curved spacetime extends this method beyond flat backgrounds. But when the metric itself must become part of the quantum dynamics, the meanings of time, causality, locality, and observables need to be reconsidered. Here, two remarkably successful descriptive languages—geometry and quantum theory—meet at their deepest level.

**Vacuum: What is present where there seems to be nothing?** In the standard cosmological energy budget, dark matter accounts for roughly 27% and dark energy for roughly 68%. Meanwhile, estimating vacuum zero-point energy with a Planck-scale cutoff leads to the cosmological-constant naturalness problem, often illustrated by a discrepancy of order \(10^{120}\). That figure depends on how the estimate is made, but it vividly conveys the tension between quantum descriptions of the vacuum and the scale of the universe.

These four questions need not share a single answer. What makes it worthwhile to consider them together is that they keep reminding us of something: **a theory can predict phenomena with extraordinary accuracy and still leave some of nature's most basic choices mysterious.**

## 5. Returning to de Broglie: A Particle's Inner Period and Its Outer Wave

In 1924, Louis de Broglie began with a daring intuition: if light can exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, might material particles also be accompanied by a wave?

Using the relativistic energy–momentum relation,

$$
E^2=(pc)^2+(m_0c^2)^2,
$$

he considered an intrinsic periodic frequency for a particle at rest,

$$
\nu_0=\frac{m_0c^2}{h},
$$

and developed the matter-wave relationships

$$
\lambda=\frac{h}{p},\qquad
v_{\mathrm{phase}}=\frac{c^2}{v}\quad(0<v<c).
$$

The phase velocity may exceed \(c\), but that does not make it a speed at which controllable information can be transmitted faster than light. The more fascinating idea is what de Broglie called *phase harmony*: although a particle's internal periodicity and its accompanying external phase wave appear with different frequencies in a moving reference frame, their phases remain in agreement along the particle's trajectory.

Let \(\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}\). Measured against laboratory time, the internal frequency along the particle's trajectory is \(\nu_0/\gamma\), whereas the external phase wave has frequency \(\gamma\nu_0\) at a fixed position. Yet at the particle's position, \(x=vt\), we have

$$
\phi_{\mathrm{wave}}(t,vt)
=2\pi\gamma\nu_0\left(t-\frac{v^2t}{c^2}\right)
=2\pi\frac{\nu_0}{\gamma}t
=\phi_{\mathrm{clock}}(t).
$$

**Two different frequencies, yet the same phase along a single trajectory.** This turns the relationship between particle and wave from the familiar phrase “wave–particle duality” into a concrete structural question: how does an internal periodicity correspond to an external wave representation?

This question also draws the attention of MST-EUT. We are exploring whether closed cycles, boundary constraints, and external projections can help us understand how a locally stable structure might appear externally as a phase mode with a definite dispersion relation. Might the conditions of internal closure impose genuine restrictions on the allowed spectrum?

The most valuable test of this direction is not to write down another equation that merely resembles \(\lambda=h/p\). It is to understand how the mass scale, phase relationships, and selection of modes might emerge from a common set of interaction conditions. Quantum measurement and probability rules, meanwhile, retain questions of their own.

## 6. Looking Back at Geometry, Quantum Theory, and Thermodynamics Through the Vacuum

If I had to choose one concept that most clearly reveals the changing ideas of physics, I would choose the *vacuum*.

In Lorentz's era, the vacuum was often imagined together with the ether that carried electromagnetic waves. Relativity removed the need for a detectable mechanical ether, but gave flat spacetime a rigorous kinematic structure. General relativity then gave the metric dynamics of its own: spacetime can have curvature and gravitational waves even in the absence of ordinary matter. In quantum field theory, the vacuum becomes a quantum state that can possess nontrivial correlations, fluctuations, and condensate structure.

The further this history unfolds, the less simple “nothingness” seems to become.

Black-hole thermodynamics makes the question even more intriguing. Beginning with the work of Bekenstein and Hawking, the area of a black-hole horizon became linked to entropy. The holographic principle and subsequent research opened new conversations among geometry, information, and quantum entanglement. Concepts that once belonged to separate fields began appearing together, again and again, in the same physical questions.

This brings Boltzmann to mind. Macroscopic thermodynamics could describe a great many phenomena long before statistical mechanics arrived. But statistical mechanics changed how we understood those laws, showing how macroscopic relationships could emerge from finer degrees of freedom and collective behavior.

Could geometry and quantum theory today be open to a comparable change of understanding?

If spacetime geometry, quantum phase, and thermodynamic information are effective representations seen at different scales, perhaps what we should seek first is not another tangible kind of “substance,” but **the mappings, couplings, and constraints preserved across different representations**. This thought leads us from the history of physics back to our present mathematical explorations.

## 7. MST-EUT: From Historical Questions to the Structure of Interactions

MST-EUT began with the hope of bringing questions temporarily set aside by successful formulas back into physical research. As the work has developed, our ideas about what a more fundamental explanation might look like have also changed.

### 7.1 From Intuitions About a Medium to Tensor Mappings and Shared Constraints

In the early stages of exploration, it was natural to begin with images of entropy fields, density, and flow. Tangible pictures help us formulate questions, but they can also tempt us to decide too early that nature must take the form of some particular medium.

Recent work on tensor dimension and order, interaction mappings, and modes of representation suggests another sequence worth trying: **first ask which relationships must be preserved; then ask what physical pictures those relationships can support.**

Here, *interaction relationships* mean linear or nonlinear mappings, couplings, and evolution among tensor variables. They do not refer specifically to the action functional of variational calculus. MST-EUT's existing Core–Boundary–Leak field variables and variational framework remain an important baseline for examining these relationships. The newer work on representations allows us to ask more carefully what belongs to the physical interactions themselves and what belongs to the language used to express them.

One physical relationship may admit different tensor decompositions; the same formula need not arise from the same generative process. **What does a mathematical representation preserve, and what does it leave hidden?** That is a question we hope to keep exploring.

### 7.2 Subjecting Shared Structures to Physical Tests

If a set of interaction relationships is genuinely more fundamental than the representations we already use, it should allow familiar physical laws to reappear under appropriate conditions.

In uniform or flat limits, we hope to find a Lorentzian causal structure shared by different channels. When the distribution of matter and fields changes, we want to understand how an effective metric can develop curvature feedback. Under conditions of local stability and boundary closure, we want to identify the permitted wave modes, phases, and spectra from the dynamics.

These questions turn philosophical curiosity into directions for mathematical work. They call for clear derivations, consistency across scales, and results that can be compared with observation. **A new description deserves to count as progress only when it genuinely increases the relationships we can understand or test.**

### 7.3 Keeping the Questions Open

MST-EUT has developed variational and operator frameworks, including Core–Boundary–Leak, and has accumulated candidate connections involving phase closure, field projections, and effective geometry. This work has gradually given the questions calculable forms, while also making the new difficulties clearer.

Why physical channels can share a causal structure, how closed modes might yield effective mass and quantum statistics, and how geometric feedback can remain consistent across scales are still questions that require step-by-step derivation. The three generations of fermions, the parameters of the Standard Model, and the microscopic origins of dark matter and dark energy remain more distant research questions.

To me, part of the joy of exploration lies in seeing which paths have begun to emerge, and which have yet to be found.

### 7.4 Letting What Seems Strange Remain a Starting Point

Planck questioned black-body radiation, Einstein reconsidered simultaneity, and de Broglie brought periodicity and waves into the description of material particles. Each of these turning points shows that scientific progress comes not only from more accurate calculations, but also from asking familiar questions in a different way.

We now possess extraordinarily successful theories of relativity and quantum phenomena. Precisely because of that success, it is worth looking carefully at which shared structures of nature they preserve—and which generative relationships may still be hidden behind the formulas.

**When a formula becomes an axiom, the exploration does not end. It simply reminds us that some questions have found remarkably good ways of being answered, while others may only now be coming into view.**
